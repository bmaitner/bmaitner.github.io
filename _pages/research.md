The Maitner Lab asks where life on Earth is, why it is there, and what is about to happen to it. We build some of the largest open biodiversity datasets and the software needed to use them, and we apply them to global change problems that need answers now, not decades from now.

## Early warnings for biodiversity under climate extremes

Climate change is pushing species into conditions they have never experienced. We map that exposure as it happens and, increasingly, before it happens. Our team built a [global early warning system](/publication/2026-06-08-serra-diaz_early_warning) that forecasts, months in advance, where species will face extreme heat (*Nature Climate Change*, 2026). We showed that in 2024, the hottest year on record, [one in six vertebrate species](/publication/2025-08-25-merow_hottest_year) was exposed to unprecedented heat across more than a quarter of its range (*PNAS*, 2025). We have also found that [high-diversity tree communities](/publication/2025-06-23-boonman_tree_exposure) face novel climates even under minimal warming. When fires swept through the Amazon and Australia, we used the same data pipelines to rapidly quantify the damage to biodiversity, including how [deregulation, drought, and fire impact Amazonian species](/publication/2021-09-01-feng_amazon_fire) (*Nature*, 2021).

## Mapping the species we know least about

Most species are rare, and rare species are the hardest to study and the most likely to need protection. About [36.5% of land plant species are exceedingly rare](/publication/2019-11-01-enquist_commonness_of_rarity) (*Science Advances*, 2019), and our knowledge of what plants are like is [strongly biased toward wealthy, well-studied regions](/publication/2023-06-27-maitner_raunkiaeran_shortfall) (*New Phytologist*, 2023). We develop [species distribution models built for small samples](/publication/2026-01-21-maitner_small_sample_sdm) (*Ecography*, 2026), so we can map species with only a handful of records.

## Open biodiversity data and software

Big questions need big, clean, open data. As part of the [Botanical Information and Ecology Network (BIEN)](https://bien.nceas.ucsb.edu/bien/), we help maintain the world's largest database of plant occurrences. I lead the [BIEN R package](/publication/2017-09-06-maitner_bien_r_package), which gives anyone free access to tens of millions of records, traits, and range maps. We also build tools that clean and standardize biodiversity data:

- [TNRS](https://cran.r-project.org/package=TNRS) standardizes taxonomic names.
- [GNRS](/publication/2022-11-14-boyle_gnrs) standardizes political division names.
- [NSR](https://github.com/EnquistLab/RNSR) flags non-native occurrence records.
- [RMMS](/publication/2019-08-28-merow_rmms) documents species distribution models so they can be reproduced.

I helped found the [Open Traits Network](/publication/2020-02-17-gallagher_open_traits) (*Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2020) and the [Open Nature Initiative](https://www.open-nature.org/), and we study open science itself. For example, we found that [only 6% of ecology papers share their code](/publication/2024-08-01-maitner_code_sharing), even though papers that do are cited at a faster rate. Our code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/MaitnerLab).

## Functional traits, from plots to the planet

The traits of organisms, such as leaf size, wood density, and body size, link species to how ecosystems function. We showed that [bootstrapping outperforms community-weighted approaches](/publication/2023-09-07-maitner_bootstrapping_traits) for estimating the shapes of trait distributions (*Methods in Ecology and Evolution*, 2023), and released the traitstrap R package to do it. Our field work spans four continents, from desert to tundra to tropical forest. I co-led international Plant Functional Traits Course expeditions in China, Svalbard, Peru, Norway, and South Africa, each bringing together 25 to 50 participants from more than 10 countries.

## Biological invasions

Why do some introduced species thrive while others fail? We developed a [framework that considers the communities species come from](/publication/2021-05-11-maitner_source_communities), not just where they land. We then showed with [introduced birds in Florida, Hawaii, and New Zealand](/publication/2021-12-24-maitner_avian_introductions) that source-region relationships consistently predict establishment success (*Ecography*, 2021).

## Global patterns of diversity

We use macroecology to explain large-scale patterns. For example, we showed that the [metabolism of warm-blooded predators explains why marine mammals and birds are most diverse in cold seas](/publication/2019-01-25-grady_marine_predators) (*Science*, 2019). With conservation partners, we have identified [areas of global importance for conserving biodiversity, carbon, and water](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-021-01528-7) (*Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2021).
